The foundation

A good plan answers
four questions — in order.

01

Does it treat the target?

Adequate dose throughout the target volume — no geographic miss, no cold spot where tumour lives.

02

Does it spare normal tissue?

Every OAR within tolerance — judged against the right dose-volume metric for that organ's architecture.

03

Is it technically sound?

Sensible isocentre, beam geometry, weighting — the plan is intentionally built, not just generated.

04

Is it deliverable & robust?

Achievable on the machine, stable to setup and anatomy variation. Undeliverable does not equal good.

"A plan is never perfect — it is the best defensible compromise. Every trade-off must be articulated. Every OAR decision documented."

The reality of plan evaluation

Bad, acceptable, trade-off - or good?

CLEARLY BAD

Unsafe or unacceptable. Easy to spot, easy to reject. Geographic miss, serial OAR breach, undeliverable MU.

ACCEPTABLE

Passes all constraints - but a better plan is clearly achievable with reasonable effort. Not wrong, but not optimal.

TRADE-OFF

Competing priorities pull against each other. Fixing one worsens another. Needs clinical judgement and documentation.

GOOD

Balanced, robust, clinically appropriate. Target covered, OARs spared, technically sound, documented. Best achievable.

THE GUT-CHECK

"Would I confidently treat my own family member with this plan?" - if the answer is not a clear yes, name exactly what would have to change.

A systematic framework

FCB-CHOPS- eight checkpoints, every plan

F
Fusion / Imaging
Verify image fusion accuracy - correct target & OAR anatomy
C
Contours
All target & OAR contours complete and anatomically correct
B
Beam Arrangement
Beam geometry and entry points appropriate for the target
C
Coverage
Prescribed dose adequately covers the target volume
H
Heterogeneity / Hotspots
Internal dose variation - acceptable high and low dose regions
O
Organs at Risk
OAR doses respect clinical constraints for each organ
P
Prescription
Correct dose, fractionation, normalisation, laterality documented
S
Summation
Cumulative dose from all courses - or explicitly acknowledge none

Start with Prescription. It defines treatment intent. Every downstream judgement flows from it - confirm it before anything else.

The letters are a safety net, not a script. Work in whatever order the plan demands - but close no plan until all eight are checked.

Block B · Beam Arrangement

Every beam should reach the target by the most direct, safest route

UNAVOIDABLE ACCEPTABLE

4-field box — beams transit OARs

Whole pelvis, bilateral treatment — OAR transit is geometry-driven. Document and manage.

AVOIDABLE RE-ANGLE

Beam transits OAR before PTV

Left lateral must pass through OAR en route to PTV. This is avoidable — enter from the other side.

SOLUTION IPSILATERAL

Enter from the OAR-free side

AP + ipsilateral lateral — OAR on the contralateral side receives no direct beam dose.

Before accepting any OAR transit — ask: is there a beam arrangement that avoids it? If yes, use it. If no, document why it is unavoidable.

BBlock 1 · Beam Arrangement & & Technical Quality

Technical red flags

RiskIsocentre in lung, air, or density interface - dose calculations unreliable. Move into stable tissue.
RiskBeam transiting directly through a critical OAR - unnecessary dose to a serial organ. Re-angle if any workable alternative exists.
RiskHot spot outside the PTV - in skin, bowel, or OAR it is unacceptable regardless of magnitude. Location, not just size.
MajorMLC clipped tight to the PTV - no penumbra margin means a cold rim on every beam. Leave 3–5 mm beyond the BEV PTV.
MajorNormalisation point in unstable tissue (lung, air, interface) - every DVH number is unreliable. Always verify before reading the DVH.

Technical Quality Check

  • Isocentre - centred in the PTV; balances field sizes; not in lung or at a density interface
  • Beam weighting - re-weight to pull the hot spot into the target and balance dose
  • Wedges - tilt the isodoses to compensate for a sloping surface or missing tissue
  • Field-in-field (FiF) - forward-planned sub-segments that block emerging hot spots; routine in breast
  • Monitor units - sanity check; excess MU adds scatter and integral dose to the whole patient

Block B · Technical Quality

Hot spots, dose gradient & integral dose

Three final technical reads - and the first is the most missed.

▶

Hot spot - location, not just size. A 107% inside the PTV is fine; in skin or an OAR it is not.

▶

Dose gradient - dose should fall steeply between PTV and any adjacent OAR.

▶

Integral dose / MU - sanity-check the monitor units; excess MU adds scatter and integral dose to the whole patient.

Don't just ask HOW BIG the hot spot is - ask WHERE it sits

Hot spot INSIDE the PTV - acceptable

Max dose sits within the target · no OAR in the high-dose region

Hot spot OUTSIDE the PTV - concern

Max dose drifted into normal tissue / OAR
→ re-weight, add wedge, move isocentre

The same hot spot is acceptable inside the PTV and a problem outside it

CBlock 2 · Target Coverage - DVH & Volumes

First: name your volumes

GTV Gross Tumour Volume - what you can see, palpate or image
CTV GTV + suspected microscopic spread - the true clinical target
ITV CTV + internal motion margin (ICRU 62)
PTV ITV/CTV + set-up uncertainty - the geometric safety margin

We prescribe to and evaluate the PTV - but the clinical aim is to cover the CTV. A small PTV-edge underdose may still leave the CTV intact.

The DVH - your primary instrument

  • Collapses a 3-D distribution into one readable curve - every point: what volume gets ≥ this dose?
  • Read up from a dose → V-metric (V95, V20 …)
  • Read across from a volume → D-metric (D95, D2 … ICRU 83 preferred)
  • No spatial information - never tells you WHERE the cold or hot sits

The DVH screens; the isodose slices diagnose. Always scroll every plane before you trust the DVH.

C/HBlock 2 · Coverage Metrics & Homogeneity

The numbers that matter

D95%

Volume of PTV receiving ≥95% of prescription. Standard aim: D95 ≥ 95%.

D2%

Near-maximum - hotspot criterion. ICRU 83: D2% <107%. Hotspot should sit inside the target.

HI

(D2−D98) ÷ D50. Lower = flatter. <0.1 good, <0.15 acceptable. SBRT deliberately exceeds this.

CI

PIV ÷ TV. Ideal 1.0–1.2. Higher expected and acceptable in 3DCRT.

Why D98 / D2 - not Dmin / Dmax?

Dmin and Dmax are single-voxel point doses - statistically noisy, shifting with grid size and contouring jitter. ICRU 83 replaced them with D98% (near-minimum) and D2% (near-maximum): clinically equivalent but stable plan-to-plan.

Coverage is negotiable

Where the PTV abuts a serial OAR, you accept a small underdose at the overlap. Safety wins. Know where the cold sits - PTV margin vs CTV is a very different problem. Radical intent demands tighter coverage than palliation.

OBlock 3 · OAR Constraints

Architecture decides the metric - getting this wrong is a class of error

Serial organs

Metric: Dmax or D1cc

One failed sub-unit = organ failure. Even a small dose spike above tolerance causes irreversible damage.

Spinal cord · Brainstem · Optic chiasm · Optic nerves

Apply a PRV margin. Evaluate dose to the PRV, not the bare organ contour.

Cord ≤45 Gy · Brainstem ≤54 Gy · Chiasm ≤54 Gy

Parallel organs

Metric: Mean dose or Vx Gy

Redundant sub-units - organ tolerates partial irradiation. Widespread low dose depletes functional reserve.

Lung · Liver · Kidney · Parotid gland · Bowel

Mean dose predicts function loss; Vx sets the toxicity threshold. Both matter.

Lung V20 <30% · Parotid mean <26 Gy · Rectum V70 <20%

Constraints are not absolutes. Re-irradiation, concurrent chemotherapy, patient comorbidities, and treatment intent all move the line. Most QUANTEC data come from ~1.8–2 Gy/fr 3DCRT - always convert to EQD₂ if fractionation changes.

Teaching Moment · Case 1

Homogeneity - why it matters and how to achieve it

Bad - no shaping

HI >0.2

No wedges - dose piles up at beam entry. Hotspot is large and often exits the PTV. Normalisation in lung makes all numbers unreliable.

Acceptable - wedges

HI <0.15

Wedge tilts the isodoses to compensate for the sloping breast surface. Hotspot <115%. Clinically deliverable and safe - FiF is not always needed.

Good - field-in-field

HI <0.10

FiF sub-segments are forward-planned to block the peak of each hotspot. Same coverage, same OAR sparing - just flatter dose distribution.

The three plans differ mainly in dose shaping - none → wedges → FiF. Coverage and OAR sparing converge across all three; homogeneity is what separates acceptable from good. Bad is bad for a different reason entirely: the normalisation point invalidated the whole DVH before you even opened it.

What the plan shows

✓Coverage: PTV well covered - target dose maintained throughout
↑Rectum: V45 / V30 elevated - high-dose isodose wraps the anterior rectal wall. This is above QUANTEC tolerance.

The core problem

The rectum is above constraint. This is the primary concern - and it drives a real clinical dilemma: coverage vs rectal sparing. What do we do?

Why is the rectum at risk?

  • The posterior pelvis - where the rectum lives - sits directly in the path of the AP/PA beams
  • The 4-field box reduces anterior bowel exposure vs a pure AP/PA plan - but the lateral fields now pass through the rectum
  • The posterior PTV margin (cervix–rectum interface) has no tissue buffer - covering it means irradiating the anterior rectal wall
  • This is a geometric constraint, not a planning error - the planner cannot move the rectum

QUANTEC rectum: V70 <20%, V50 <50% (late rectal bleeding ≥ grade 2). For 45 Gy whole-pelvis, the relevant constraints shift to lower dose volumes. What is the threshold - and is it exceeded here?

What Can We Do? · Case 2

Managing rectum dose in whole-pelvis irradiation

1 - Adjust field weighting

Increase the contribution of the lateral fields relative to AP/PA. Spares the anterior rectum - but increases lateral small bowel and femoral head dose. Every adjustment has a consequence: state it.

2 - Patient positioning & preparation

Full bladder protocol displaces small bowel superiorly. Prone position + belly board displaces the anterior bowel out of the field. These are the most powerful single interventions - not field arrangement alone.

3 - Margin reduction at the posterior PTV

If the CTV–rectum relationship is clearly documented on imaging, a reduced posterior margin at the rectum overlap may be justifiable. The CTV must still be covered - document explicitly.

4 - Adjust the prescription (if clinically appropriate)

In selected cases - particularly where the rectum or bowel constraint is the binding limit - a modest dose reduction or hypofractionation adjustment may allow the constraint to be met without sacrificing CTV coverage. This requires a genuine clinical discussion: is the original dose biologically necessary, or is there room to negotiate? Senior input required.

5 - Accept with documentation

If coverage cannot be maintained while meeting all OAR constraints, the trade-off must be explicitly documented: which constraint is exceeded, by how much, the clinical justification, and senior sign-off. The written record - not just the plan - is what protects the patient and the team.

Don't forget the boost

EBRT is phase 1. Brachytherapy adds significant rectal and bladder dose. Track cumulative dose from the start - the EBRT plan that looks acceptable alone may push over constraint once the boost is added. Summation planning is not optional.

What the plan shows

✓Coverage: CTV D98 ≈ 99.2% · PTV D98 ≈ 96.6% - good and maintained
↑Rectum: V55 ≈ 37.9% · V59 ≈ 32.9% - over tolerance at the posterior PTV margin

Two OARs over tolerance

Rectum V55 and penile bulb mean are both above constraint - and they're in different anatomical locations. Are these fixable? Or is this a fundamental geometric problem?

Why does this happen?

  • The posterior PTV margin (prostate–rectum interface) has no tissue buffer - covering it exposes the anterior rectal wall to high dose
  • The penile bulb lies immediately inferior to the prostate - a low inferior PTV margin pulls the high-dose region over it
  • Both OARs are downstream of target coverage decisions - the root cause is geometric proximity, not planning error
  • Reducing dose to either OAR would directly compromise CTV coverage at the relevant margin

QUANTEC: Rectum V70 <20%, V50 <50%. Penile bulb: Dmean <52 Gy, D90 <50 Gy. Both are exceeded. What is the priority - and what can realistically be changed?

What Can We Do? · Case 3

Managing rectum and penile bulb dose - the posterior-margin problem

1 - Reduce the inferior margin

A targeted reduction of the inferior PTV margin (where the penile bulb is exposed) can significantly reduce penile bulb mean dose - without affecting posterior rectal dose. Verify CTV inferior extent is still fully covered. Gain: penile bulb. Risk: inferior CTV miss if margin was clinically justified.

2 - Reduce the posterior margin

A reduced posterior PTV margin at the prostate–rectum interface (if CTV coverage is maintained) lowers rectal dose - but this is where microscopic disease lives. Requires imaging evidence that CTV is clear of the rectal wall. Not always possible.

3 - Adjust posterior oblique weighting

Reduce relative weight of posterior beams - redistributes dose anteriorly. Reduces posterior rectal dose but may increase anterior OAR (bladder, small bowel) contribution. DVH check required after any re-weighting.

4 - Adjust the prescription (if clinically appropriate)

Where the penile bulb or rectal constraint is the binding limit, a modest reduction in total dose or a change in fractionation may reduce OAR exposure without a critical loss of tumour control. This is a genuine clinical option - not a default - and requires senior discussion, biological rationale (EQD₂ comparison), and documented consent. Not always available, but always worth considering explicitly.

5 - Accept with explicit documentation

If coverage cannot be maintained while meeting all constraints, this must be documented as an accepted trade-off: which OAR is over tolerance, by how much, the clinical rationale (curative intent requires posterior coverage), and senior sign-off before treatment approval.

The bigger lesson

Coverage was good. The problem was the OARs. A plan that covers the target but exceeds OAR constraints is not a good plan - it is a plan with an identified problem that needs a decision. The evaluator's job is to name the problem, not ignore it.

The mandatory item

The optic chiasm at 54.9 Gy is above a hard serial OAR limit. This plan cannot be approved without explicit written justification and senior sign-off. Can it be improved?

Why is this so hard?

  • The pituitary fossa sits immediately below the optic chiasm - there is often no tissue gap between PTV and chiasm
  • Any beams that cover the superior PTV also pass through or very near the chiasm
  • This is an intrinsic geometric tension, not a planning error
  • Coplanar beams all enter from similar angles - all of them contribute to the chiasm dose without any geometrically sparing arrangement

The chiasm is a serial organ - Dmax is the relevant metric, not mean. Even a small volume above 54 Gy constitutes a tolerance breach. What could we change about the beam arrangement?

Case 5 · H&N · Findings

What the plan shows

✓Coverage: PTV High mean ≈ 66.0 Gy - target well covered
↑Contralateral parotid mean ≈ 3.30 Gy - above what we'd expect; is this acceptable?
✓Spinal cord max 19.9 Gy · Brainstem max 18.2 Gy - both within tolerance
~Submandibular (R) mean ≈ 40.4 Gy - elevated; improvement may be achievable
NoteSummation: electron/photon match - cumulative dose at the junction must be verified
The key question

Contralateral parotid at 3.30 Gy isn't catastrophic in absolute terms - but it's higher than necessary. Why is it receiving dose at all? And could it be reduced without sacrificing anything? What is causing it?

Why is the contralateral parotid receiving dose?

  • The 4-field plan includes a right lateral field that enters through the ipsilateral (affected) side - acceptable, as the tumour is there
  • But this same beam exits through the contralateral parotid - the exit dose irradiates it directly, regardless of MLC shaping
  • You cannot block the exit of a beam with an MLC - the beam has already deposited dose as it exits. This is a geometry problem, not a shaping problem.
  • The only solution is to remove the beam that exits through the contralateral parotid
The clinical stake

Xerostomia is the most common serious late effect of head and neck RT. Even modest reductions in contralateral parotid mean dose translate to meaningful differences in long-term QoL and patient-reported dry mouth. This is not a minor concern.

Wrap-up · Key takeaways

What to carry forward

1
FCB-CHOPS every time. Work in whatever order the plan demands - but close no plan until all eight boxes are checked.
2
DVH screens; slices diagnose. No spatial information. Always scroll every plane before you trust the DVH.
3
Match metric to organ architecture. Serial → Dmax / D1cc. Parallel → mean or Vx Gy. Getting this wrong is a class of error.
4
Normalisation point matters. In lung, air, or at a density interface - every DVH number is unreliable. Verify before you read anything.
5
Document every trade-off. The conversation and the written justification are as important as the plan itself.
