The central question

A good plan answers four questions

01

Does it treat the target?

Adequate dose to the whole target — no geographic miss, no cold spot where tumour lives.

02

Does it spare normal tissue?

Every OAR within tolerance — judged against the right dose-volume metric for that organ.

03

Is it technically sound?

Sensible isocentre, beam angles, weighting — the plan is built, not just generated.

04

Is it deliverable & robust?

Achievable on the machine, stable to setup variation. Undeliverable ≠ good.

"A plan is never perfect — it is the best defensible compromise. Every trade-off articulated. Every OAR decision documented."

A systematic framework

FCB-CHOPS — a checklist for every plan

F
Fusion
Image fusion accuracy — correct target & OAR anatomy
C
Contours
All target & OAR contours complete and correct
B
Beams
Beam geometry and entry points appropriate for the target
C
Coverage
Prescribed dose adequately covers the target volume
H
Hotspots
Dose variation — acceptable high and low dose regions
O
OARs
OAR doses respect clinical constraints
P
Prescription
Correct dose, fractionation, normalisation documented
S
Summation
Cumulative dose from all courses — or acknowledge none

Start with Prescription — it defines intent. Work in whatever order the plan demands; the letters are a safety net, not a script.

Block 1 · Target Coverage

The DVH — your primary instrument

  • Every point answers: what volume gets at least this dose?
  • Read up from a dose → V-metric (e.g. V95)
  • Read across from a volume → D-metric (e.g. D95)
  • ICRU 83 prefers D-metrics — statistically stable
  • No spatial information — never tells you WHERE the cold or hot sits

The DVH screens; the isodose slices diagnose. Always scroll before you read the DVH.

Coverage — key numbers

D95

≥95% of prescription. Near-minimum for the PTV.

D2

<107% (ICRU 83). Near-maximum — hotspot criterion.

HI

(D2−D98) ÷ D50. <0.1 good, <0.15 acceptable.

CI

PIV ÷ TV. Ideal 1.0–1.2; higher expected in 3DCRT.

Coverage is negotiable

Where PTV abuts a serial OAR — accept a small underdose at the overlap. Safety wins. Know where the cold sits: PTV margin vs CTV reach are very different problems.

Block 2 · OAR Constraints

Architecture decides the metric

Serial organs

Metric: Dmax or D1cc

One failed sub-unit = organ failure. A small dose spike above tolerance causes irreversible damage — spinal cord, brainstem, optic chiasm, optic nerves.

Apply a PRV margin. Evaluate the PRV, not the bare organ.

Hard limits: Cord ≤45 Gy · Brainstem ≤54 Gy · Chiasm ≤54 Gy

Parallel organs

Metric: Mean dose or Vx Gy

Redundant sub-units — organ tolerates partial irradiation. Widespread low dose depletes functional reserve — lung, liver, kidney, parotid.

Mean dose predicts function loss; Vx sets the toxicity threshold.

Examples: Lung V20 <30% · Parotid mean <26 Gy · Rectum V70 <20%

Mismatching the metric is a class of error. Reading Dmax off a parallel organ, or only checking mean dose on a serial organ, gives a false sense of safety. Match the organ architecture to its metric, every time.

Triage + your task

Sort every finding into a tier

CriticalRe-plan required — geographic miss · serial OAR above hard limit · hot spot in critical organ
MajorCorrect or justify — parallel OAR over constraint · V95 90–95% · avoidable beam through OAR
MinorComment only — slightly high MU · non-ideal isocentre · minor inhomogeneity
Reject on sight if…

1 Cold dose reaching into the CTV  ·  2 Serial OAR exceeds a hard tolerance  ·  3 Plan is unsafe or undeliverable

Teaching Moment · Case 1

Why homogeneity matters — and how to achieve it

Bad — no shaping

HI >0.2

No wedges, no compensation for the sloping breast contour. Dose piles up at the beam entry; hotspot is large and often exits the PTV.

Acceptable — wedges

HI <0.15

Wedge tilts the isodoses to compensate for the sloping surface. Hotspot <115%. Clinically deliverable and safe — FiF not always needed.

Good — field-in-field

HI <0.10

FiF sub-segments are forward-planned to block the peak of each emerging hotspot. Same coverage and OAR sparing — just flatter dose.

The three plans differ mainly in dose shaping — none → wedges → FiF. Coverage and OAR sparing converge; homogeneity separates acceptable from good. Bad is bad for a different reason entirely: the normalisation point invalidated the whole DVH.

Teaching Moment · Case 2

The posterior margin — where coverage and rectal sparing conflict

Why the rectum is always at risk

The posterior PTV margin (prostate–rectum interface) has no tissue buffer. Any meaningful coverage of the posterior prostate exposes the anterior rectal wall. This is the fundamental geometric tension in prostate planning — it cannot be engineered away, only managed.

Penile bulb — why it matters

The penile bulb lies immediately inferior to the prostate. High dose (mean >52 Gy) is associated with erectile dysfunction — a significant long-term QoL concern, especially in younger patients. At 62.8 Gy mean, this plan exceeds the QUANTEC limit substantially.

QUANTEC — the working reference

Rectum: V70 <20%, V50 <50% (primary limit for late rectal bleeding ≥ grade 2). These constraints come from conventionally fractionated 3DCRT data. If you change fractionation, convert to EQD₂ — never read a 2 Gy/fr number onto a hypofractionated plan.

What to do with this plan

Document the trade-off explicitly. Senior discussion before approval. Consider whether inferior margin reduction preserves penile bulb; verify posterior margin coverage doesn't fall below CTV. The conversation — not the plan alone — is what matters here.

Teaching Moment · Case 3

Pituitary fossa — proximity, hard limits & non-coplanar geometry

Chiasm at 54.9 Gy — a hard limit case

The pituitary fossa sits immediately below the optic chiasm. PTV and chiasm are often adjacent or overlapping — coverage vs chiasm sparing is an intrinsic geometric tension, not a planning error. At 54.9 Gy (limit 54 Gy), explicit justification and senior sign-off are mandatory. Document the reasoning, not just the number.

Air cavity undercoverage — expected

The sphenoid sinus has near-zero electron density. Undercoverage (88.7%) is physics-driven — not a planning failure. Document it, reassure the team, and resist the urge to over-dose adjacent structures to compensate.

Why non-coplanar fields help

Non-coplanar beams enter across a wider range of skull-base angles, spreading the entrance dose and reducing the contribution from any one direction. The result: better inferior PTV coverage (+2.1 Gy), lower brainstem mean (−1.6 Gy), and reduced integral brain dose — without worsening the chiasm, because the chiasm is a geometric constraint independent of the beam arrangement.

The lesson

Some OAR findings cannot be optimised away — they are geometric facts. The planner's job is to recognise them, document them, and make the best plan possible within the constraint — not pretend they don't exist.

Wrap-up · Key takeaways

What to carry forward

1
FCB-CHOPS every time. The framework is a safety net — work in whatever order the plan demands, but close no plan until all eight boxes are checked.
2
DVH screens; slices diagnose. No spatial information. Always scroll the isodoses on every plane before you trust the DVH.
3
Match metric to organ architecture. Serial → Dmax / D1cc. Parallel → mean or Vx Gy. Getting this wrong is a class of error.
4
Normalisation point matters. In unstable tissue — lung, air, density interface — every DVH number is unreliable. Always verify.
5
Document every trade-off. The conversation — OAR above tolerance, clinical justification, senior sign-off — is as important as the plan itself.
